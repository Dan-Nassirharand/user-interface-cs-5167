## Affordances

The most commonly perceived affordances of shoes are as follows:

- Wearable
- Displayable
- Preservable

In cultures where shoes are worn, the affordances of the object are categorized primarily between utility and aesthetics, the specific intersection of which depends on the taste and needs of the consumer per instance of shoe.

There are, of course, as with any object, affordances that are less commonly perceived by users and forgone by designers. Such examples include

- Ability to be thrown
- Ability to be used as a decoration
- Ability to be used as a device to transport water
- Ability to be used as a device to store items

### Wearable

Perhaps the most perceivable affordance of shoes is that they may be worn on one's feet.
While there are many ways to actually wear a shoes (slip-on, velcro, straps, laces, etc.), there is generally a portion of empty space on the top of the shoe where a consumer is meant to insert their foot, leading with their toe.

Due to the longstanding history of shoes in the world, this affordance is easily learned by consumers of various backgrounds and does not necessitate the use of signifiers on part of the designer.

### Displayable

Whereas wearability has more to do with the utility of shoes, the displayable affordance of shoes is categorized more by its aesthetic applications, such as expressions of taste and emotion via the art form of fashion.

This affordance, due to the presence of shoe culture in many first world countries, is commonly perceived by users in proportion of their participation in said culture.

### Preservable

Some might confound the perservable quality of shoes with its capacity to be displayed, however it is more so an affordance between that of being displayable and wearable on the scale of utility and aesthetics, leaning towards one end of the spectrum based on the demographic of the user and the shoe itself.

Common, multi-purpose shoes lend themselves to be preserved from a financial perspective (ex. a student buying one pair of shoes a year), whereas special-purpose shoes, like those used for ballet and rock climbing, may be preserved with a performance constraint in mind. A public figure in New York might want to preserve their shoes as a way to telegraph social status to those around them.

Preservability is an affordance characterized not only by the shoe itself, but that of the intersectionality of the user and cultural aspects therein.

## Physical Properties

The physical properties of shoes are as follows:

- Portable
  - The functional lifetime of the object is spent in motion as an extension of the user's body
- Small
  - About the size of a human foot
- Rigid
  - Depends on the type of shoe, but most tend to have a softer upper-layer and a harder bottom towards the sole
- Free-standing
- Light
  - Often less than a pound. Specialty shoes, such as those used for construction work or skiing can exceed 1 pound.
